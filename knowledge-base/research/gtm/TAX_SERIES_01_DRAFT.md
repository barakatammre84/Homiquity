Your tax return is the most honest document in your financial life. You signed it under penalty of perjury, it captures every income stream you have, and — unlike a pay stub — it shows the version of your income you chose to report. That is exactly why mortgage underwriting leans on it so heavily, and why understanding what a lender sees in it can change how you prepare to buy a home.

## The 1040 is a map, not a number

A lender does not read your tax return for the bottom line. They read it for the story: where income comes from, how stable each stream is, and which parts count toward a mortgage.

- Wages on line 1 are the easy part — they get cross-checked against W-2s
- Interest, dividends, and capital gains raise a question: recurring, or one-time?
- Schedule C, Schedule E, and K-1 income each trigger their own analysis
- Adjustments and deductions tell the underwriter how aggressive your tax strategy is

Two people with identical total income can qualify for very different loan amounts because of how that income appears across these lines.

## If you are self-employed, the return IS the application

W-2 employees prove income with pay stubs. Self-employed buyers prove it with tax returns — usually two years of them. Here is the part almost nobody explains before it hurts: the write-offs that lower your tax bill also lower the income a lender can count. Deduct aggressively for two years, and on paper you earn what is left after deductions, not what your business actually brings in. Underwriting adds some things back (depreciation is the classic example), but most deductions permanently reduce your qualifying income.

This is not a reason to overpay taxes. It is a reason to know the trade-off exists — ideally two years before you want to buy, when there is still time to plan for both goals.

## What underwriters add back, and what they do not

Some paper losses get added back to your qualifying income because no cash actually left your pocket:

- Depreciation and depletion
- Business use of home
- One-time casualty losses
- Amortization

Other deductions are treated as real, ongoing costs and stay subtracted — vehicle expenses, supplies, contract labor, meals. The distinction is cash flow: underwriting tries to reconstruct the money that actually reached you.

## Declining income is a bigger flag than low income

Two stable years at a modest income reads better than a great year followed by a weaker one. A meaningful decline between year one and year two invites questions and can cap qualifying income at the lower year — or below it. If your income dipped for a documented one-time reason, the explanation belongs in the file, and the earlier you surface it the better.

## What to do with this

- Keep business and personal finances cleanly separated — commingling muddies the analysis
- If a home purchase is one to two years out, talk to your tax preparer about the deduction trade-off before filing, not after
- Gather two years of complete returns — every schedule, every K-1 — before you talk to any lender; missing pages are the most common avoidable delay
